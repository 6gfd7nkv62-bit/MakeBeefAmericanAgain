Government record · Ruling N354481

The government already answered the origin question.

In October 2025, U.S. Customs and Border Protection ruled on American chicken that had been shipped to China, cooked, and sent back. Customs held that it never stopped being a product of the United States.

Read the holdingRead the full ruling letter
The holding

Not substantially transformed.

“In the case of the above-described Frozen Grilled Chicken Skewers, this office finds that the article is not substantially transformed by the processing that occurred in China. Accordingly, based on the information presented, the finished goods are products of the United States.”U.S. Customs and Border Protection, Ruling N354481, October 28, 2025

American raw chicken was exported to China, where it was deboned, tumbled, cut, skewered, steamed, chargrilled or fried, frozen and packaged — then imported back into the United States for sale to wholesalers.

Customs found that none of it created a new article. The chicken kept its American origin.

The contradiction

Two products. Two standards.

ProductWhat is done to itThe government’s position
American chickenDeboned, tumbled, cut, skewered, steamed, fried and frozen in China, then imported backStill a product of the United States — Ruling N354481
Foreign beefCut, ground and repackaged in the United StatesIts foreign identification can come off under USDA’s reading of 9 CFR § 327.14

Both products are processed. The chicken is processed far more heavily — cooked, breaded, fried — and Customs still holds that it is American. The beef is merely cut and wrapped, and USDA allows its foreign origin to vanish.

One agency guards an American origin against heavy processing abroad. The other lets a foreign origin dissolve under light processing at home.

The standard

The test, in the agency’s own words.

Country of origin is defined at 19 CFR § 134.1(b). A good is a product of the country where it is manufactured, produced or grown — and later work changes that only if it effects a substantial transformation.

The test is whether the article emerges with a new name, character or use, per Texas Instruments Inc. v. United States, 69 C.C.P.A. 151 (1982). Customs decides on the totality of the evidence, per National Hand Tool Corp. v. United States, 16 C.I.T. 308 (1992).

That is the same standard our letter asks USDA to apply to imported beef.
